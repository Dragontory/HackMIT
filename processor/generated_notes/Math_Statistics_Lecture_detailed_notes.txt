## Data Types and Visualization Methods

### Categorical/Qualitative Data
* Represents qualities, categories, or groups
* Cannot be meaningfully averaged or summed
* Examples: department (humanities, management), gender, rank

#### Visualization Method: Bar Chart
* **Purpose**: Shows frequency or count for each category
* **Example from dataset**:
  - 12 professors teach humanities courses
  - 9 professors teach management courses

> **Visualization Tip**: Bar charts should have clearly labeled axes and categories. The height of each bar represents the frequency or count of observations in that category.

### Numeric/Quantitative Data
* Represents measurable quantities
* Can be mathematically manipulated (averaged, summed)
* Examples: salary, years of experience, age

#### Visualization Method: Histogram
* **Purpose**: Shows the distribution of numeric data by dividing it into intervals (bins)
* **Example from dataset**:
  - Only 1 professor received a salary between 1.6 and 1.8 (units likely in $100,000s)
  - 9 professors received salaries between 2.6 and 2.8

> **Practical Application**: Histograms help us quickly identify patterns in data distribution that might not be obvious from looking at raw numbers.

## Describing Distributions

Understanding the shape of a distribution helps us choose appropriate statistical methods and make better inferences about our data.

### Common Distribution Patterns

1. **Unimodal Distribution**
   * Has one peak (one most common value)
   * Example: Bell-shaped normal distribution

2. **Uniform Distribution**
   * No peaks; all values occur with approximately equal frequency
   * Example: Rolling a fair die (each number 1-6 has equal probability)

3. **Bimodal Distribution**
   * Has two peaks (two commonly occurring values)
   * Example: Test scores in a class with two distinct groups of students

### Symmetry and Skewness

1. **Symmetric Distribution**
   * Left and right sides mirror each other
   * Mean and median are approximately equal
   * Example: Normal distribution

2. **Right-Skewed Distribution (Positively Skewed)**
   * Longer tail extends to the right
   * Mean is greater than median
   * Example: Income distributions, where a few high earners pull the mean upward

3. **Left-Skewed Distribution (Negatively Skewed)**
   * Longer tail extends to the left
   * Mean is less than median
   * Example: Age at death in developed countries

> **Key Insight**: The shape of a distribution affects which measure of central tendency best represents the "typical" value. For skewed distributions, the median is often more representative than the mean.

## Measures of Center for Numeric Variables

These statistics help us identify the "typical" or "central" value in our dataset.

### Mean (Average)
* **Definition**: Sum of all observations divided by the number of observations
* **Formula**: x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₙ)/n
* **Example**: For the dataset {1, 3, -5, 4, -2}
  - Sum = 1 + 3 + (-5) + 4 + (-2) = 1
  - n = 5
  - Mean = 1/5 = 0.2

* **When to use**: Best for symmetric distributions without outliers
* **Limitations**: Sensitive to extreme values (outliers)

### Median
* **Definition**: The middle value when data is arranged in order
* **How to find**:
  1. Arrange all observations in ascending order
  2. If n is odd, the median is the middle value
  3. If n is even, the median is the average of the two middle values

* **Example**: For the dataset {1, 3, -5, 4, -2}
  - Arranged in order: {-5, -2, 1, 3, 4}
  - n = 5 (odd)
  - Median = 1 (the middle value)

* **When to use**: Better for skewed distributions or when outliers are present
* **Advantage**: Not affected by extreme values

> **Study Tip**: When a distribution is symmetric, the mean and median are approximately equal. When skewed right, mean > median. When skewed left, mean < median.

## Measures of Spread for Numeric Variables

These statistics tell us how dispersed or varied our data is.

### Variance
* **Definition**: Average of squared deviations from the mean
* **Formula**: s² = Σ(xᵢ - x̄)²/(n-1)
* **Interpretation**: Larger values indicate greater spread in the data
* **Note**: We use (n-1) in the denominator for sample data to get an unbiased estimator

### Standard Deviation
* **Definition**: Square root of the variance
* **Formula**: s = √s²
* **Advantage**: Expressed in the same units as the original data
* **Example calculation process**:
  1. Calculate the mean
  2. Subtract the mean from each observation
  3. Square each difference
  4. Sum the squared differences
  5. Divide by (n-1)
  6. Take the square root

> **Practical Application**: Standard deviation is widely used in statistics to measure dispersion. In a normal distribution, approximately 68% of observations fall within one standard deviation of the mean.

### Interquartile Range (IQR)
* **Definition**: The difference between the third quartile (Q3) and first quartile (Q1)
* **Formula**: IQR = Q3 - Q1
* **How to find quartiles**:
  1. Arrange data in ascending order
  2. Find the median (Q2)
  3. Q1 is the median of the lower half (values below the median)
  4. Q3 is the median of the upper half (values above the median)

* **Example**: For the dataset {1, 3, -5, 4, -2, 2, -1, 10}
  - Arranged in order: {-5, -2, -1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 10}
  - Median (Q2) = (1+2)/2 = 1.5
  - Lower half: {-5, -2, -1, 1}
  - Q1 = (-2+-1)/2 = -1.5
  - Upper half: {2, 3, 4, 10}
  - Q3 = (3+4)/2 = 3.5
  - IQR = 3.5 - (-1.5) = 5

* **When to use**: Particularly useful when outliers are present
* **Advantage**: Not affected by extreme values

> **Key Insight**: The IQR contains the middle 50% of the data and is the basis for identifying outliers in boxplots.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Data Types Matter**: Different types of data (categorical vs. numeric) require different visualization and analysis techniques.

2. **Distribution Shape Influences Analysis**: Understanding whether data is symmetric, skewed, unimodal, or bimodal helps determine appropriate statistical methods.

3. **Multiple Measures Provide Insight**: Using both measures of center (mean, median) and measures of spread (standard deviation, IQR) gives a more complete picture of your data.

4. **Context Is Critical**: Always interpret statistics in the context of the dataset and research question.

5. **Visualization Enhances Understanding**: Bar charts, histograms, and other visual tools help identify patterns that might be missed in raw data.

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. Using the mean for highly skewed data
2. Ignoring outliers without investigation
3. Choosing inappropriate bin sizes for histograms
4. Applying parametric tests to non-normally distributed data
5. Drawing conclusions beyond what the data supports
